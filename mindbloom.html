Mind Bloom

A merge puzzle for two players who almost never share a game - a seven-year-old and their grandparent. Merge flowers, clear seventy rounds, and spend everything you win building a garden that stays yours - on iOS and Android.

Role Product & UX Designer
Timeline 6 weeks
Players Children 6-12 & adults 45+
Platform Mobile game, iOS & Android
Overview

In a nutshell

Mind Bloom is a merge puzzle where everything you combine grows into something better - three daisies make a fern, ferns make clovers, clovers bloom. Each round states one goal in plain words at the top of the board and gives you a fixed number of moves to reach it. Clear the round and the coins you earned drop into a garden that persists between sessions: seventy rounds across seven worlds, and the garden at the end is the thing you actually built. It's designed for two audiences most puzzle games treat as one - children who want the next round immediately, and players past 45 who would rather sit with a board and think.

The Problem

Built for everyone, designed for no one

Merge games are easy to download and easy to abandon. Most are tuned for a single imagined player - young, quick, playing in short bursts - and everyone outside that profile quietly drops off. Designing for children and for players over 45 at the same time meant re-examining defaults the genre had stopped questioning.

Two audiences

children and players 45+, sharing one grid and one set of rules

70 rounds

difficulty has to climb across seven worlds without once feeling unfair

One goal

everything on the board has to serve a single, stateable objective

Tutorials nobody finishes

Children skip them and older players feel talked down to. The first board has to teach the rule by being playable.

Targets tuned for young thumbs

Small tiles and tight timers quietly exclude the 45+ player the game is also being made for.

Score with nowhere to land

Coins and stars are abstract. Nothing accumulates into something a player can point at and say they made it.

Difficulty that spikes

One unreadable round late in a world is where most players stop - and stopping once is usually stopping for good.

"Make the first move obvious and the fortieth move interesting." - the rule I came back to every time a round felt either patronising or punishing.
Designing for
two ages

Designing for a seven-year-old and a sixty-year-old at once meant no decision could be justified with "most players". Each one had to hold up twice.

What the two audiences need
What it changed in the design
Players over 45 often play at arm's length, one-handed, in whatever light they're sitting in.
Tiles differ by shape as well as colour - never colour alone - on targets big enough to hit without aiming.
Children replay a round they have already cleared; older players almost never do.
Rounds stay open and replayable, but garden rewards drop only once. Replaying is for enjoyment, not grinding.
Both groups quit at a round they can't read, not at a round that is hard.
Every board opens with at least one obvious merge available, so the first move is never a guess.
Why a garden and not a score : a score resets the moment you close the app. A garden doesn't. Putting the reward somewhere permanent is what turns "one more round" into a reason to open the game tomorrow - and it gives a child and a grandparent the same thing to be proud of.

5.1

One goal, in words, at the top

Every board opens with a sentence rather than a symbol - Collect 3 Clovers - and the moves you have left sit directly beside it. There is no timer, no chat, no second currency to track mid-round. The board is the only thing asking for attention.

5.2

Merging up a tier

Three of a kind become one of the next tier: daisies to ferns, ferns to clovers, clovers to a bloom. Each tier has its own silhouette, so a player who can't easily separate two greens still knows exactly what they are holding.

5.3

Reading your progress without leaving the board

Progress lives in the same strip as the goal, so checking how far along you are never costs a tap. Higher tiers are drawn louder than the base tile - the thing you are working toward is always the thing that stands out.

5.4

Round complete, garden grows

The completion screen itemises where every coin came from - merge rewards, completion bonus, stars - instead of flashing one total. Then the map shows the next round already unlocked, and everything just earned goes into the garden.

Impact

What the design is built to move

Mind Bloom is pre-launch, so the honest way to talk about impact is what the design is engineered to change rather than numbers it hasn't earned yet. Each of these traces back to a specific decision on the board.

↑ Reach

one game two generations can play, with no separate "kids mode"

↓ Drop-off

at the late-world rounds where a run usually ends for good

↑ Return

a garden that is worth opening the app for tomorrow

↓ Frustration

moves are limited, time never is, and no board opens unreadable

Reflection

What stuck with me

The instinct with a 45+ audience is to design a gentler copy of the same game - bigger text, fewer rules, an easy mode sitting off to one side. That would have made a worse game for everybody and a slightly patronising one for them. What actually worked was making a single game legible enough that nobody needs the concession: shape carrying meaning alongside colour, one goal per board, and difficulty that climbs instead of jumping. The seven-year-old benefits from every one of those decisions too.

Designing for a child and a grandparent at the same time forced every decision to be defensible twice - and the game got simpler each time.
