01 Context

A crowded search page

"Mobile app development company in Dubai" is one of the most contested searches in the region. Page one is a wall of agencies saying the same thing: years of experience, number of apps, a "get a quote" button.

The business needed pages that would rank, so they had to cover the topic better than competitors, and convert, so a CTO or founder would trust the page enough to share their phone number. Pages in this category usually manage one of those, not both. Long keyword-stuffed pages rank but don't convert, and polished brochure pages convert the few visitors who ever find them.

The brief, reframedDon't design "a landing page". Design the page that answers every question a buyer in this market types into Google, in the order they ask it, with proof right next to each claim.
02 Research

Reading the search, not just the brief

Before wireframing I went through the pages already ranking for each keyword, the "People also ask" questions, and Clarity recordings of our existing service pages. Three patterns stood out:

Intent

Buyers search for cost, compliance and proof

The questions people actually ask are "how much does it cost", "is it PDPL compliant", "have you built this before". Generic service lists answer none of them.

Behaviour

Most visitors never reach the form

On our old pages, recordings showed visitors scrolling past services and leaving before the contact block at the bottom.

Market

Every market trusts different signals

Dubai buyers look for UAE PASS and CBUAE readiness, Saudi buyers for Arabic RTL, Nafath and Vision 2030, US buyers for HIPAA, CCPA and SOC 2.

03 Decisions

Five calls that shaped every page

01

Conversion goes in the hero, not the footer

Every page opens with the headline keyword, three trust chips and a short lead form side by side. The form asks for four things, and the copy underneath answers the two worries people have about forms: "reply within 24 hours" and "NDA available".

code-brew.com/sa/mobile-app-development…
02

One H2 for every question buyers ask

I mapped the page outline to search intent: services, results, industries, cost, compliance, process, tech stack, FAQ. Each H2 answers one query cluster, and the H3 cards below it are short enough to scan. The FAQ blocks (10 to 36 questions per page) pick up long-tail searches.

Page outline · Dubai
  • H1: Custom Mobile App Development Company in Dubai
  • Proof: logos, stats, Clutch 4.9/5
  • Services for UAE businesses (8)
  • Results: 6 client cases with metrics
  • Industries (8) → Devora cost estimator
  • Testimonials (8) → AI-powered development
  • UAE compliance: PDPL, DIFC, UAE PASS, CBUAE
  • Tech stack → cost → FAQ (10) → final form
03

Proof inside the first two scrolls

Client logos, four big numbers and award badges sit right under the hero. Further down, every results card leads with a metric, such as "35% fewer missed appointments" or "55% faster chart retrieval" on the healthcare page.

04

Local proof, not a translated template

The four pages share components but not content. Riyadh gets Arabic RTL, Nafath, Mada, STC Pay, ZATCA and Vision 2030. The healthcare page gets NPHIES, Seha and HIPAA alignment. New York gets NY SHIELD, CCPA/CPRA and ADA/WCAG 2.1 AA. Each compliance grid is a checklist a procurement team can screenshot.

05

Turn "how much does it cost?" into a lead

"Cost" is one of the biggest intents behind these searches. On the Dubai page I put Devora, the AI cost estimator I designed, in that section. It asks four questions and gives an estimate in 60 seconds. Visitors get an answer, and the sales team gets a lead with the full project context.

Rankings are a team result. The SEO and content teams did the keyword strategy and the writing. My part was making sure the structure, hierarchy and conversion path worked for both readers: the crawler and the person with a budget.

06 Reflection

What stuck with me

Good SEO design is mostly good UX: answer the question someone typed, in the order they'd ask it.

If I did this again I'd set up conversion tracking per section before launch, not after. Then I could show which blocks (estimator, compliance grid, results) actually move people to the form, and cut the ones that don't.
